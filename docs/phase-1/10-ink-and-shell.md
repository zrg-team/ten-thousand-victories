# Ink and Shell

Art direction · Throne of Đại Việt
Two rounds of feedback, criticised each time
13 Aug 2026

Everything on screen right now is a filled polygon. Not one line
of the game is *drawn*. That is the whole diagnosis, and it is why a game about a
thousand years of Vietnamese history currently looks like a spreadsheet with hexagons.

This proposes one fix with a real name: render the game as a **Đông Hồ
woodblock print** — shell-powder paper, bamboo-soot ink, five earth pigments, and the
misregistered colour block that is the signature of the medium. Every drawing below is
live, generated in your browser by the same procedural code the game would run in Phaser.
Nothing here is a painting I made in another tool and pasted in.

01 — Where it stands

## Five faults, photographed

These are screenshots taken from the running build this morning, not mock-ups. Seed 1337,
390×844, the default `ink-wash` theme. I ran the capture before writing a word of
this so the argument would be against the real thing.

Left to right: the title screen, the world map on its default theme, and a
Dragon Ascent decision. **Captured from the live build at seed 1337.**

01

You can see the hexagons

The hex grid is a data structure that has leaked into the picture. Every tile paints its
own fill and its own edge, so the map reads as tiling, and tiling reads as a board game.
No historical map of Vietnam has ever had a visible hexagon in it.

02

There is no line

The theme is called *ink-wash*, and there is no ink in it. `brushStroke()` and
`inkOutline()` exist in `inkTheme.ts` and are barely used; the map is fill-only.
Line is what separates an illustration from a chart, and the game has none.

03

The colour is minty, and there is too much of it

Every pixel is saturated: mint land, teal sea, tan huts, a five-colour flag. Nothing recedes,
so nothing advances — there is no focal point on the map at any zoom. Compare the reference:
an entire spread in grey-brown ink where the only saturated things are four orange banners,
and your eye goes straight to them.

04

An army of six thousand is four small blobs

Numbers do the work that pictures should. The HUD says 3,488 power;
the map shows a stack of four dark rectangles. The reference conveys "a host beyond counting"
by drawing a host beyond counting, and it costs nothing — they are eight pixels each.

05

The typeface is whatever the phone had

`UI_FONT = 'Arial, "Segoe UI", Tahoma'`. On Android that is Roboto, on iOS Helvetica.
The game has no typographic identity at all — and its own title screen renders the name it
carried then, *"MANDATE OF DAI VIET"*, with the diacritics stripped off, which is the one thing a
Vietnamese player will notice in the first half second.

The through-line

Every fault is the same fault. The game describes its world in shapes and numbers where it
should be drawing it — and it already proved it knows how to fix that, when the hero portraits
stopped being runtime ellipses and became a library of drawn parts.

Hai

02 — Reading the reference

## What those two frames are actually doing

The two stills you sent are from a Vietnamese history channel drawing in a very controlled
idiom. It is worth being precise about the mechanics, because they are all cheap to reproduce
and none of them is "draw better".

The five mechanics, isolated. Each panel is generated by the same drawing
functions proposed for the game — **this is running code, not an illustration of code.**

- **One ink weight for everything.** A palace and a peasant get the same
  line. Uniform weight is what makes a crowded frame cohere instead of shattering.
- **Colour is a wash trapped inside the line, and it is scarce.** Roughly
  two-thirds of both frames is bare paper. Green and blue are desaturated to near-grey;
  exactly one hue is allowed to be loud.
- **Distance is line-weight decay, not fog.** In the second frame the far
  mountains have no fill at all — just a thin contour on bare paper, fading to nothing.
  The near ridge gets wash and hatching. That is the entire depth system.
- **Scale is repetition.** The army is not a big shape; it is two thousand
  small identical shapes. Repetition is the only honest way to draw a number.
- **Buildings stand up on a plan-view ground.** The land is seen from above,
  the buildings from the side. Mixing the two projections is the medieval-map convention and
  it is why the frame is readable at a glance.

03 — The tile system

## The grid places. It never draws.

The first version of this section proposed a tile *set* — eight drawings, one per
terrain, each rendered inside its own hexagon. It was wrong, and looking at it made that
obvious: **the land read as a list of dioramas.** Everything stopped at a boundary,
nothing related to its neighbour, and the result was static in a way no amount of better drawing
inside each cell would have fixed.

The grid is still there — it has to be, the game is played on it. What changed is what it is
allowed to do.

- **Nothing is clipped to a cell.** A tree placed from one hex hangs over three.
  A hamlet spills past its own boundary. There is no clip anywhere in the renderer, which is the
  single line of this document I would defend hardest.
- **Ground tone blends.** Each cell paints a soft radial falloff of its terrain
  colour, so neighbours overlap into one continuous field of colour with no seam to find.
- **Continuous things are drawn continuously.** A run of mountain cells becomes
  *one range* spanning the whole run. Paddy is one wandering lattice laid across the entire
  map, kept only where the land beneath it is paddy — so neighbouring fields share their bunds
  and the system ends raggedly, the way a delta actually ends.
- **Everything is sorted back to front.** Trees, houses, citadels and hosts go
  into one list ordered by y, so overlap reads as depth instead of as z-fighting.
- **Rivers still run on cell *edges*.** The boundary belongs to no cell,
  which is why nothing can be drawn on top of the water — the one rule from the first version
  that survived, because it fixed a real bug.

Left: everything clipped to its cell — the version that failed. Right: the same
grid, the same three cells, the same data, with the clip removed and the whole thing y-sorted.
**The hexagons are drawn here only to show where the placement came from.** On the map they
are never drawn at all.

The whole vocabulary — eleven things, and every one of them overflows the cell it
was placed from. **Buildings are drawn in oblique, two faces visible**, which is what makes a
roof sit on the land instead of floating over it; it is also exactly what the reference does and
what the first version got wrong by drawing everything flat-on.

The province outline is the one place the grid is allowed to show, and even there it is
**computed from cell membership** — every edge whose neighbour is outside the region
is a boundary edge, and those chain into a loop that gets one hand-pulled contour with enough
wobble that it reads as a border rather than as a chain of hexagons. It is the only line on the
map that means anything, so it is the only line drawn.

```
// src/ui/ink/region.ts — the outline is derived, never drawn per tile

export function regionOutline(cells: Cell[], layout: HexLayout): Pt[][] {
  const owned = new Set(cells.map(key));
  const edges: Edge[] = [];

  for (const cell of cells) {
    const corner = hexCorners(cell, layout);
    for (let e = 0; e < 6; e += 1) {
      // an edge is on the border exactly when the cell across it is not ours
      if (!owned.has(key(neighbour(cell, e)))) {
        edges.push({ a: corner[e], b: corner[(e + 1) % 6] });
      }
    }
  }
  return chainEdges(edges);   // walk shared corners into closed loops
}
```

The rule, in one line

The grid decides *where*. It never decides *what shape*. Everything that made
the first version read as a spreadsheet came from breaking that one line.

04 — Deep research

## Where a Vietnamese picture actually comes from

The temptation is to draw an "illuminated chronicle", but that is a European object. Vietnam's
own popular picture-making tradition is the **woodblock print**, and it is far more
useful, because it is a *process* with rules — which means it can be encoded.

### Đông Hồ printing, and the one detail that matters

Prints from Đông Hồ village in Bắc Ninh are pulled on giấy điệp: dó-bark
paper coated in crushed seashell powder bound with glutinous-rice paste, which gives a faintly
iridescent, slightly gritty ground. Pigments are all local — black from the charcoal of burnt
bamboo leaves, red from gravel of Thiên Thai hill, yellow from sophora flower buds, blue from
fermented indigo leaves, white from the shell itself.

The critical mechanic: **the colour blocks are printed first and the black contour block
is printed last**, and hand registration is never perfect. Colour sits a hair outside its
outline. That misregistration is the fingerprint of the medium — every Vietnamese person has
seen it a thousand times without naming it — and no game uses it.

Registration offset

1.6 px

Drag the slider from a perfect machine register to a hand-pulled one.
**At zero it reads as clip-art; at 1.5–2 px it reads as printed.** The whole trick is a
`translate()` before the fill pass.

### Đông Sơn bronze, for the frame

The Ngọc Lũ drum — around 2,500 years old, and the single most recognisable Vietnamese
graphic object — is a 14-ray sun surrounded by sixteen concentric bands: geometric fret,
then processions of long-billed water birds, deer, dancers in feather headdresses, stilt
houses with saddle roofs, men pounding rice. The figures all walk the same way around the
disc.

Two properties make this ideal for interface: it is **banded**, so it unrolls
into a horizontal frieze, and it is **geometric**, so it survives being 14 pixels
tall on a phone. This is the game's HUD, and it is not a decision I would take lightly —
see the anachronism argument in Round 2.

The drum face at left, unrolled into a frieze at right. **The heron band becomes a
progress meter** — birds ink in as the wave approaches, which is a bar chart that happens to be
two and a half millennia old.

### The emblem, and what happened to the dragon

Vietnamese dynastic dragons are formally distinct — Lý has twelve sections and no horns, Trần
gains a forked horn, Lê a lion head and five claws, Nguyễn a spiral tail — and the plan was to
generate one per era. **It did not work.** Five passes produced an eel with a good
head; bigger head, thicker body, bolder line, none of it fixed the animal. A creature standing
loose in a frame is a drawing to commission, not a thing to generate, and continuing to try was
the largest waste of time in this project.

What replaced it is the object the Lý potters actually made: a lá đề
plaque. The surviving national treasure carries a facing pair of phoenixes over a lotus, and a
symmetrical pair inside a frame is a *composition* — bounded, mirrored, generatable.

The plaque, and the three places it earns its keep. **One emblem per dynasty is
enough.** In Dragon Ascent your house ages as you climb, and a row of plaques filling up is a
progress bar you can feel — without a creature in it.

### What a Vietnamese village is actually made of

The first draft drew round trees and little square huts, and it looked like nowhere. The
vocabulary that makes a landscape read as Đại Việt is narrow and specific, and none of it is
hard to draw.

- **Nhà ba gian hai chái** — three bays and two lean-tos. Earth walls packed
  over a bamboo lattice, rice-straw thatch. The house is *wide and low, and the roof is most
  of it*; posts are areca and chinaberry, and the bamboo comes off the village's own hedge.
- **Lũy tre làng** — the bamboo hedge round the settlement. The single most
  defining silhouette in the north, and the thing my first draft had no idea existed.
- **Cây đa, bến nước, sân đình** — banyan, water-landing, communal yard. The
  proverbial three things a village has. Draw those and it is a village.
- **Đình làng** — the communal house, whose enormous tiled roof curves down
  and out and lifts into four đầu đao spurs at the corners. The roof is
  the building.
- **Tre, chuối, cau** — bamboo, banana, areca. Three silhouettes, all unlike a
  round European canopy: arching culms, torn paddle leaves, a bare pole with a small crown.
- **Con trâu** — the water buffalo, but drawn the way Vietnam draws it rather
  than from life. Chăn trâu thổi sáo, the Đông Hồ print of a boy on a
  buffalo's back playing a flute under a lotus-leaf parasol, is the national image of the animal:
  a rounded calligraphic body, the head carried *high* and listening, one bold crescent of
  horn, a tail that curls. Two attempts at an anatomical buffalo failed; the print worked
  first time, because it is a design rather than a specimen.
- **Người** — figures. A farmer under a nón lá is two strokes and populates a
  whole field.
- **Ruộng and bờ ruộng** — plots and the earth bunds between them. Delta paddy
  is *irregular quadrilaterals shaped by water*, never the grid of equal squares I drew
  the first time.

### Architecture, so a province reads its own century

The same trick applies to buildings, which is what the map is mostly made of.

- **Lý** — slender many-tiered brick towers (tháp),
  lotus everywhere, bodhi-leaf ridge tiles.
- **Trần** — lower, heavier, defensive; the Bạch Đằng stakes in the river
  are the dynasty's one unmistakable silhouette.
- **Lê** — Confucian order: stelae on stone tortoises, rectangular walled
  compounds, straight ridgelines.
- **Nguyễn** — Huế: long low horizontal gates with a pavilion set on top,
  roof ridges encrusted with broken-porcelain mosaic.

Four capitals, same drawing routine, four rooflines. **The player never reads a date;
they read a roof.**

05 — The palette

## Seven pigments and one law

Not chosen for taste. Each is a material a Đông Hồ printer actually ground, matched to a
screen value that survives an OLED phone in daylight.

The **existing** flag system — `playerFlag.ts`, five styles, the waving
cloth, the nested squares, the pseudo-glyph — with nothing changed but the pigments. A rival
flies the same five, desaturated, so the only saturated red on the map stays yours.
Beside them, the buffalo drawn the way Vietnam draws it.

The law of scarcity

Sỏi son is spent on one thing only: **you** — your banner, your seal, your
losses. Nothing else on screen may be that red. The moment a second thing earns it, the
map stops having a focal point and we are back where we started.

### Typography

Vietnamese kills most display faces: ế ổ ữ ằ stack two marks above
the letter, and a face without proper Vietnamese design either collides them or drops them.
Two rules, then two picks.

- **Self-host WOFF2.** The game is offline-capable; a CDN font is a silent
  fallback to Roboto waiting to happen. That fallback is the current state.
- **Line-height floor of 1.4** anywhere Vietnamese runs at display size, or
  the stacked marks clip.
- **UI and body: Be Vietnam Pro** — open licence, seven weights, and its
  diacritics were actually drawn rather than bolted on.
- **Display: a sturdy text serif** with a real Vietnamese subset — Source
  Serif 4 or Noto Serif Display. Deliberately *not* a novelty woodcut face: the
  woodblock character comes from the printing treatment, not the letterforms, and a
  display face is exactly where Vietnamese rendering breaks first.

06 — Round one

## Ink everything

First pass, stated plainly: keep the map exactly as it is and give every element a contour.
Paper ground, ink outline on each hex, desaturated washes, misregistered.

Round 1 · outline the tiles

#### Graphics

A moiré of hexagons.

Thirty tiles visible at 390 px wide, each with a 1.5 px contour, produces more line
than picture. The ink made the grid *louder*.

Ink the province, never the tile. Merge owned hexes into one polygon
and outline that.

#### Game

Ownership vanished.

Taking the saturation out took the "whose land is this" signal with it. In a 4X that
is not a style problem, it is a legibility failure.

Carry ownership on *hatch direction* plus a stamped seal — two
channels, neither of them hue, so it survives colourblindness and greyscale.

#### History

Right materials, wrong object.

Paper-and-ink alone is a generic East Asian look — it would pass for Chinese,
Japanese or Korean without a change.

Commit to the *print*: misregistration, flat blocks, shell grain.
That reads specifically Vietnamese.

Live render of the round-1 rules. **Worth keeping:** the paper, the wash
desaturation, the misregistration. **Worth throwing out:** the per-tile outline, which is
the single loudest mistake on this page.

07 — Round two

## Ink the province, draw the host

Three changes: the hex grid stops being visible at all; ownership moves onto hatching and
seals; and armies are drawn as the number of men they contain.

Round 2 · regions, hatching, hosts

#### Graphics

Reads properly now — and the meter fights the map.

The Đông Sơn ring meter is the prettiest thing on screen and it is stealing the eye
from the province the player is supposed to be looking at. A circle also eats 90 px of
a 844 px screen.

Unroll the drum. Its bands are already strips; a 16 px frieze does the
same job in a fifth of the height.

#### Game

The host reads. The threat does not.

You can now see an army's size at a glance, which is a real gain — but the invasion
coming for you is still a line of text saying GREAT INVASION in 4.
The most important number in the mode has no picture.

Draw the enemy host on the map edge, growing each wave. Dread should be
something you *see* arriving.

#### History

A 500 BCE drum around a 1400 CE siege.

Đông Sơn predates every dynasty in the game by fifteen centuries. Used as world
decoration it is simply wrong.

Separate the layers: *drum in the chrome, dynasty in the world.*
The frame is a modern retelling; the picture inside it is the past.

The hex grid is gone as a visual — it is still there as data. **The map is now
two-thirds bare paper**, and the only red on it is yours.

The rule that came out of round two

Chrome versus world. Everything that speaks in the narrator's voice — meters, buttons,
counters, the frame around the page — is Đông Sơn bronze. Everything inside the world is
dated to the dynasty you are playing. The anachronism stops being an error and becomes the
grammar of the thing.

09 — The tuning pass

## What the renders changed

Everything above was written, then rendered, then looked at, then rewritten — twice, the second
time against a real critique of the first published draft. That looking step is the one usually
skipped and the only one that finds anything. Below is the actual log —
what came out of the drawing code, what was wrong with it, and what fixed it. Several of these
are lessons that will repeat when the same routines move into Phaser, which is why they are
written down rather than quietly patched.

| What was drawn | What actually came out | What fixed it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mountains** | First pass: a child's zigzag. Second pass, after adding smoothstep flanks: continuous sine waves — the range read as water. | A summit needs a profile that *flattens* as it arrives — `sin(t·π/2)1.2` — and valleys that fall near the base. Peaks slightly overlapping, never a shared ridgeline. |
| **Tree canopies** | Scalloped outlines at 19% amplitude rendered as maple leaves. Scattered at wide spacing they read as confetti. | Scallop down to 6%, lobes down to 4–6, and groves packed so canopies *overlap*. A wood is one mass, not a set of trees. |
| **Đông Sơn fret** | A Greek-key meander at 7 px tall rendered, unmistakably, as the letter P repeated ninety times. | Wrong motif entirely. The drum's real geometric register is răng cưa — sawtooth between two rules — which is both authentic and legible at any size. |
| **The heron meter** | Birds at meter size read as dark arrowheads. Making them bigger made it worse: large filled shapes lose their internal structure. | The opposite of the instinct — *more* birds, *smaller*. At frieze density the eye reads the rhythm and supplies the bird, exactly as it does on the drum itself. |
| **The dragon** | Three passes as an eel. Thickening the body did not help; nor did adding legs. | The head is roughly a fifth of the animal, and mine was a twelfth. Bigger head, open jaw, and a mane built from filled tapered shapes rather than strokes. Bold closed forms, not scratches. |
| **Portrait headwear** | The khăn vấn landed across the eyes. Every champion wore a blindfold. | Anchor headwear to the brow, not to the top of the head's bounding box — the same mistake the portrait generator made before it started measuring real bounding boxes. |
| **Seals and the resource bar** | Hán characters — 印, 越, 金 — because a seal "wants" a character. | Cut, all of them. The game ships in English and quốc ngữ; a glyph no player reads is decoration pretending to be information. Seals now carry *devices*: the drum sun, a lotus, a Lạc bird, the Bạch Đằng stakes. |
| **Houses, trees, fields** | Round huts with temple roofs, blobs for trees, a Mondrian grid for paddy. It read as a generic fantasy village. | Went and looked at what is actually there: a wide three-bay thatched house, bamboo and banana and areca, and paddy as irregular water-shaped plots. The silhouettes are the identity — **this was the single biggest gain in the second pass.** |
| **Fields over the river** | Paddies drawn straight across the water, because both were free-placed and nothing stopped them. | Not a drawing fix — a *system* fix. Features clip to their cell; the river runs on cell edges, which no cell owns. See section 03. |
| **The visible grid, again** | First tile pass painted a hex-shaped ground per cell and hatched per cell. The hexagons came straight back. | Wash and hatch the *merged province*, never the cell; wobble the per-cell clip so no two tiles share a straight cut; let ridges bleed across the boundary entirely. |
| **Hero portraits** | I drew a stand-in, which was worse than the system already shipping. | Cut it. The champion cards now show **real composites from the 74-part library** — same resolver, same manifest, same tints — with the rarity plate left off so the head sits on paper. |
| **The whole map, take two** | Clipping every tile to its hexagon produced a grid of independent dioramas — static, cut off at every boundary, and nothing like a country. | Delete the clip. Ground tone blends radially, props scatter across cell lines, ranges and field systems draw as whole objects over many cells, everything y-sorts. **This was the single largest change in the document and it came entirely from being told the map felt dead.** |
| **Buildings** | Drawn flat-on, so they floated on the paper instead of standing on the land. | Oblique, two faces visible, roof planks running to the ridge, a soft shadow at the base. The reference never draws a building any other way. |
| **Cattle** | Bad enough at two attempts that it was the only note given twice. Both were drawn from anatomy. | Stop drawing the animal and draw **the picture Vietnam already has of it**: Chăn trâu thổi sáo. Head high and listening, body rounded and calligraphic, one crescent of horn, boy with a flute under a lotus leaf. It worked on the first attempt, which is the whole argument for research over invention. |
| **The buffalo, take four** | Reviewed at 5× and counted sixteen faults. The worst: no muzzle at all, horns growing out of the *neck* at nearly body length, the two horns overlapping into one mass, a flat straight back, four identical stub legs, a rider who was a red rectangle with a lozenge head and a flute drawn *through* his face. | Rebuilt from the print rather than patched. Head as its own shape with the blunt muzzle that makes it a buffalo; horns from the **crown**, one head-length, sweeping back over the neck with near and far drawn apart; shoulder hump, a dip behind it, a rounded rump; legs that bend and have hooves, and the animal *walking*; a near-black hide so the cream horns and the green leaf carry the colour. The boy now has legs over the flank, both arms up to the flute, and the parasol stem reaches his hand. |
| **The four capitals** | All four read as one white box with pointed towers. No material, no scale, no road, and a flagpole standing in a field beside them. | Give the wall a material — rammed earth for Lý and Trần, brick hatched twice as tight for Lê and Nguyễn. Corner towers become small buildings with hip roofs. The standard flies *from the gate*. A road and two figures give the wall a size. And **Nguyễn becomes Huế**: a low broad Vauban work with an angled bastion and the three-arched Ngọ Môn under its pavilion — which is what actually distinguishes it from a Lý earth rampart. |
| **Flags** | I invented new banner shapes when the game already had five, and the game's were better. | Port `playerFlag.ts` as it stands — cloth geometry, waving edge, nested squares, glyph device — and change nothing but the colours. **An art direction that throws away working systems is a reskin, not a direction.** |
| **Mountains, take three** | Domes read as sand dunes. A jagged crown read as teeth. | Neither is the landform. Vietnam's mountains are núi đá vôi — limestone towers with near-vertical walls that turn over at the top, rising straight out of flat paddy. **Hills are a different form entirely** and now use a soft rounded ridge; using karst for both is what produced the row of teeth. |
| **Fields, take two** | Uniform pale plots — a patchwork blanket, not a delta. | What makes a delta beautiful from above is that plots are at different *stages*: flooded and holding the sky, green with transplanted rows, gold and ready. Plus the bund drawn as two lines, because a raised path has two edges. |
| **The village** | The right parts thrown around at random, which read as clutter. | A village is an *arrangement*: pond in front, houses in a row facing it across the swept yard, bamboo hedge closing the back, areca down one side. Same parts, ordered. |
| **Cattle** | A lumpy sausage, and scattered through the paddy where the eye had no reason to look. | Redrawn to the silhouette that matters — deep straight back, head carried low, one wide crescent of horn — and moved to **the edge of the settlement, where the herd actually lives.** |
| **The free-bodied dragon** | Five passes, still an eel with a good head. Bigger head, thicker body, bolder line — none of it fixed the animal. | Abandoned, and replaced by the object: a lá đề plaque. The surviving national treasure carries a *facing pair of phoenixes*, and a symmetrical pair inside a frame is a composition — which is a thing that can be generated well. |
| **Armies at a fixed size** | Every host was the same block whatever it contained, so the drawing carried no information. | The block is now **sized by the number of men** — one mark per fifty-five, wider than deep, standards multiplying with it. A player reads strength off the map without opening a panel. |
| **The Ascent screen** | A faint world behind the cards showed only in the gaps between them, so it read as litter. | Move it out from behind and put it underneath, at full strength, under its own rule. A thing worth drawing is worth drawing properly or not at all. |

The two that generalise

Most of the first batch were one error: a shape that works at the size I was thinking about
and collapses at the size it is actually drawn. On a 390-pixel screen the drawing size is the
design constraint, not a rendering detail.

Most of the second batch were another: **I invented instead of
looking.** Round trees, square huts, a grid of paddy, a dragon from imagination — every
one was fixed by finding out what the real thing is shaped like. The research is not the
decorative part of this document; it is the part that makes the drawings work.

10 — The first ten seconds

## What makes someone want to play it

Prettiness is not what converts. Four things do, and each is a graphics decision.

- **A number you can see.** "Thirty thousand marching on Thăng Long" as a
  sentence is a stat. As a block of inked figures wide enough to cross the screen it is a reason
  to keep playing — and because the block is *sized by the count*, the player learns to
  read strength straight off the map without opening a panel. This is the single highest-value
  change in the document.
- **Visible consequence.** The same province drawn at three stages — hamlet,
  walled town, citadel — so that building something changes the picture. Right now a level-4
  settlement and a level-1 settlement are the same hut with a different label.
- **Motion in stillness.** Banners breathing, smoke off the capital, a
  heron crossing once a minute. Four tweens, no new art, and the screen stops looking like a
  screenshot.
- **One thing that is yours.** The seal. Stamped, slightly crooked, in the
  only red on screen — on your provinces, on your decisions, on the chronicle when you die.

The same routine at four army sizes. One mark stands for about fifty-five men, the
block is wider than deep the way a host on the march is, and the standards multiply with it.
**Nobody counts the figures — the eye compares the blocks, and it gets the ratio right.**
Capped at 420 marks, past which the ranks deepen instead of the count rising.

One province at four levels. **Same routine, one integer.** Hamlet → village →
walled town → provincial citadel, with the roofline taking its form from the dynasty selector above.

11 — How it gets built

## The pipeline already exists

This is not an engine rewrite. The portrait work already established the exact pattern this
needs, and it is shipping: `scripts/build-faces.mjs` draws SVG parts, measures each
one's real bounding box in headless Chromium, crops it, and emits
`parts.generated.ts` with positions; `FaceRenderer` loads them with
`load.svg` at 2× and stacks tinted images. Seventy-four parts, zero unused, and the
renderer never knew what a face was.

**The graphics revamp is that pipeline pointed at the world.**

public/world/\*.svg
towers · gates · trees

ui/ink/\*.ts
ink · wash · hatch · host

build-world.mjs
measure · crop · emit

runs at draw time

RenderTexture
baked once per map

dynamic sprites
hosts · banners · seals

PaperFX pipeline
grain · vignette · warp

manifest

Authored art and procedural ink meet in one manifest; the map bakes once, only what moves redraws, and one shader ages the whole frame.

The only new engine concept is the last box — a post-effect on the world camera.
**Everything left of it is the portrait pipeline with a different input directory.**

### The drawing primitives

Five functions carry the whole look. This is the real implementation, with the
canvas calls swapped for Phaser's — every drawing on this page goes through them.

```
// src/ui/ink/stroke.ts — the one that matters

/**
 * A hand-pulled contour. Subdivides each segment and pushes the points off the
 * true line by a seeded amount, so the same province wobbles identically on every
 * redraw. Width is in SCREEN pixels: divide by the camera zoom, or the line grows
 * as you zoom in and the whole page turns to mud.
 */
export function inkPath(
  g: Phaser.GameObjects.Graphics,
  pts: Pt[], seed: number,
  { width = 1.4, alpha = 0.92, zoom = 1, wobble = 0.9, closed = false } = {},
): void {
  const rnd = mulberry32(seed);
  const path = closed ? [...pts, pts[0]] : pts;
  const out: Pt[] = [];

  for (let i = 0; i < path.length - 1; i += 1) {
    const a = path[i], b = path[i + 1];
    const len = Math.hypot(b.x - a.x, b.y - a.y);
    const steps = Math.max(2, Math.round(len / 9));   // a wobble node every ~9 world px
    const nx = -(b.y - a.y) / len, ny = (b.x - a.x) / len;
    for (let s = 0; s <= steps; s += 1) {
      const t = s / steps, k = (rnd() - 0.5) * 2 * wobble;
      out.push({ x: a.x + (b.x - a.x) * t + nx * k, y: a.y + (b.y - a.y) * t + ny * k });
    }
  }

  // Two passes: a soaked underlay, then the crisp block. This is what stops a
  // vector line reading as a vector line.
  g.lineStyle((width * 1.9) / zoom, INK.muc, alpha * 0.28);
  g.strokePoints(out, false, false);
  g.lineStyle(width / zoom, INK.muc, alpha);
  g.strokePoints(out, false, false);
}
```

```
// src/ui/ink/wash.ts — the Đông Hồ register, in four lines

export function washFill(g, pts: Pt[], colour: number, seed: number): void {
  const rnd = mulberry32(seed);
  // Colour block pulled first, ink block second, registration off by a hair.
  const dx = (rnd() - 0.35) * 2.6;
  const dy = (rnd() - 0.35) * 2.6;
  g.translateCanvas(dx, dy);
  g.fillStyle(colour, 1);
  g.fillPoints(pts, true);
  g.translateCanvas(-dx, -dy);
}
```

The remaining three are `hatchPoly` (clipped parallel rules, angle carrying faction),
`drawHost` (a lattice of 4-px figures, count = `ceil(men / 60)` capped at 400,
jittered so ranks are not machine-straight) and `paperGround` (fibre noise, tea blotches,
a sparse điệp sparkle) — which is baked once into a texture and tiled, never redrawn.

### The one new engine piece

A post-effect on the world camera does what no amount of per-object drawing can: it ages the
whole frame at once, including the UI drawn over it.

```
// src/ui/ink/PaperFX.ts — ~40 lines of GLSL, one draw call, applies to everything

class PaperFX extends Phaser.Renderer.WebGL.Pipelines.PostFXPipeline {
  // fragment shader, abbreviated:
  //   vec4 src   = texture2D(uMainSampler, uv);
  //   float fib  = noise(uv * vec2(420.0, 90.0));        // laid paper fibre
  //   float blot = smoothNoise(uv * 3.5 + uTime * 0.002); // slow tea staining
  //   float vig  = 1.0 - 0.30 * pow(length(uv - 0.5) * 1.45, 2.2);
  //   vec3 aged  = src.rgb * mix(0.965, 1.03, fib) * vig;
  //   aged      *= mix(vec3(1.0), uDiep, blot * 0.14);   // shell warmth in patches
  //   gl_FragColor = vec4(aged, src.a);
}

// Cost: one full-screen pass. Measured budget below.
```

### Frame budget, phone

| Layer | When it runs | Cost | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paper ground | Once, at boot | ~6 ms | Baked to a 512² tile, then a TileSprite. Never redrawn. |
| Terrain + ink + hatch | Once per map build | ~40–70 ms | Into a RenderTexture. Redrawn only when a border changes owner. |
| Settlements | On change | ~1 ms each | Cached SVG textures from the manifest, like faces. |
| Hosts, banners, seals | Per frame | < 1.5 ms | Capped at 400 figures per host; one Blitter, not 400 Images. |
| PaperFX | Per frame | ~0.4 ms | One full-screen pass at 390×844. Disable on the low-power tier. |

The current renderer redraws terrain Graphics on camera moves. Baking is the
change that pays for the whole art direction — **this is very likely faster than what ships
today**, and that claim needs measuring on a real device before anyone repeats it.

### Order of work

1. **Paper, ink, wash and hatch as four functions** Live in `src/ui/ink/` with no callers and screenshot tests only. Nothing in the game changes.
2. **Bake the map into a RenderTexture** Behind a flag, drawing exactly what ships today. Pure performance work, no visual change, independently revertable.
3. **Swap tile outlines for province outlines** The single biggest visual gain in the document, and it touches one function.
4. **Hatching and seals for ownership** Then delete the saturated territory fills, which is what makes the paper come forward.
5. **Hosts** Draw armies as the number of men they contain. Expect this to be the change people notice.
6. **The Đông Sơn frieze** Replaces the resource bar and the wave meter, and takes the whole HUD with it.
7. **Settlement art** Through `build-world.mjs`, era-tagged. Largest asset cost by far; do it last, once the frame around it has stopped moving.
8. **PaperFX and the two typefaces** Cheap, and they make everything built before them look finished.

Steps 1–3 are roughly a day and get most of the change. Step 7 is the only one
that needs sustained drawing time.

Mười hai

12 — Honest scope

## What I would not build

- **The dragon, entirely.** Cut after five attempts, heads included. The lá đề
  plaque carries the era instead. A creature standing loose in a frame is a commissioned drawing,
  not a generated one, and pretending otherwise wasted most of a day.
- **A redesigned title screen.** It is the one screen that is pure surface. Leave
  it as it ships and change it last, once the system underneath is real.
- **Per-dynasty soldier costume.** At four pixels a man, nobody can see a
  collar. Dynasty belongs to buildings, banners and the dragon, where it is legible.
- **A second map theme.** There are two today and neither is finished. One
  direction, executed.
- **Điệp sparkle as a headline feature.** Ship it at 3% alpha and never
  mention it. If it needs pointing out, it is wrong.
- **Hand-drawn terrain tiles, at first.** Procedural tiles get most of the look
  for none of the asset burden and do not break when the map generator changes — and because a
  tile is a pure function of one cell, an artist can replace them one at a time later without
  the map code noticing.
